When Small Districts Disappear: The Fiscal Effects of Arkansas’s Act 60 Consolidation Mandate

with Josh B. McGee · EDRE Working Paper, University of Arkansas [No. TBD] · Presented at AEFP, SREE, and ABFM 2026

Act 60 required Arkansas districts with fewer than 350 students to merge. Comparing 59 merged districts with 179 that never merged, I find no statistically significant change in total spending per pupil.

Beneath that, spending moved between categories: general administration fell by about $105 per pupil, while instructional staff support, transportation, and pupil services rose. Administrative savings were offset by higher transportation and staff costs.

Read the full summary

Background. After the 2002 Lake View ruling declared Arkansas school funding inadequate and inequitable, Act 60 required districts with fewer than 350 students to merge by 2005, on the promise that larger districts would save money on administration. Through 2020, 85 districts merged away and 105 school campuses closed, mostly local high schools. The consolidated districts were mostly small, rural, and poor.

Design. I ask whether overall spending fell, where the money moved, and whether the fiscal effects lasted. Using a 21-year panel (2000–2020) of inflation-adjusted per-pupil expenditures, I compare 59 treated districts with 179 similar districts that never merged, using Gardner’s (2022) two-stage difference-in-differences estimator.

Findings. Consolidation produced no statistically significant change in total (−$20), current (+$19), or capital (−$56) spending per pupil. Beneath that, spending moved between categories. General administration fell by about $105 and instruction by about $92 (marginally significant), while instructional staff support rose by about $109, transportation by $56, and pupil services by $37. Administrative savings were offset by higher transportation and staff costs. Event-study estimates trace these effects over the 16 years after a merger. The administration and instruction estimates show marginal joint pre-trends, so causal claims for those categories require caution.

2026 · Discover Psychology, 6(1), 93

Sarker, M. J. A., Maranto, R., Haque, A., & Sagar, M. S. U. (2026). Social media impacts on Bangladeshi college students.

Using survey data from 683 undergraduate students at a university in northern Bangladesh, this study uses logistic regression and structural equation modeling to examine how social media use relates to depression, anxiety, and stress (measured with the DASS-21). Time on social media was not directly related to any of the three outcomes. It was linked to them indirectly, through study hours, romantic and friendship crises, and career anxiety.

2025 · PLOS ONE, 20(3), e0317567

Sarker, M. J. A., Hasan, M., Kabir, A., & Haque, A. (2025). Leveraging artificial intelligence to assess the impact of COVID-19 on the teacher-student relationship in higher education.

Using a survey of 401 students at public and private universities in Bangladesh, who recalled three periods (before, during, and after COVID-19), this study applies K-Modes clustering and Non-negative Matrix Factorization, along with a new Absolute Rate of Fluctuation measure, to track changes in the teacher-student relationship. The relationship deteriorated during the pandemic, most in class conduct, exam-taking, and assessment, and less in personal interaction, student support, and collaborative research.

2022 · Frontiers in Psychiatry, 13, 938230

Hossain, M. T., Islam, M. A., Jahan, N., Nahar, M. T., Sarker, M. J. A., Rahman, M. M., et al. (2022). Mental health status of teachers during the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic: A web-based study in Bangladesh.

This web-based survey of 381 school, college, and university teachers in Bangladesh, conducted during the second COVID-19 wave in August 2021, measured depression, anxiety, and stress with the DASS-21. Prevalence was 35.4% for depression, 43.7% for anxiety, and 6.6% for stress. Residence, institution type, self-reported health, social and electronic media use, and fear of COVID-19 were significantly associated with mental health in regression models.

Hossain, M. T., Ahammed, B., Jahan, N., Islam, M. A., Rahman, M. M., Khan, B., Sarker, M. J. A., et al. (2022). Prevalence and predictors of pornography exposure during the third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic: A web-based cross-sectional study on students in Bangladesh.

This web-based survey of 646 school, college, and university students across all eight divisions of Bangladesh (June–July 2022) estimates how common pornography exposure was and what predicts it, using chi-square tests and multiple logistic regression. Overall, 75.9% reported exposure in the past two months. Exposure was more likely among older students and those married or in a relationship, and less likely among those who lived alone, held anti-pornography attitudes, or reported higher religiosity.

Under Review

Under review · Telematics and Informatics Report

Sarker, M. J. A., Hasan, M., Kabir, A., Haque, A., & Hamja, A. A data-driven analysis of the education-employment linkage with explainable artificial intelligence.

Using survey data from students and professionals across multiple Bangladeshi universities, this study applies machine learning to predict employment and Explainable AI tools (SHAP, Shapash, and LIME) to identify which factors matter most. Boosting classifiers performed best, with accuracy, F1, Kappa, and MCC scores between 94% and 96%. Perceived academic skills, non-academic factors such as soft skills, traditional cultural mindsets, self-confidence, and techno-linguistic competence emerged as the most influential predictors. These are model-based associations, not causal effects.

"Merging for Efficiency? Causal Evidence from Arkansas School District Consolidation"

Sarker, M. J. A., & McGee, J. B.

This paper asks how well Arkansas districts use their dollars. I compare each district with the most efficient Arkansas districts and ask what explains the differences. Similar student performance appears possible with about 26% fewer resources, though context matters. The gap began to widen in 2021, and all four efficiency clusters, defined by 2016 efficiency scores, were less efficient in 2024 than in 2016. Districts that devote a larger share of spending to general administration, maintenance, and transportation tend to be less efficient, while those that devote more to food, school administration, and student support tend to be more efficient. Districts serving more special education, low-income, and nonwhite students also tend to be less efficient, which may reflect the higher cost of meeting greater needs. These estimates are correlational, not causal.

Earlier versions presented at AEFP 50th Annual Conference (2025) and SREE 2025.

"The Leadership Multiplier: How Leadership Shapes the Meaning of School Dollars"

Sarker, M. J. A., McGee, J. B., & Maranto, R.

Using a panel of Arkansas school districts from 2009 to 2019, this paper follows how revenue becomes learning in two steps: how state, federal, and local dollars are routed to instruction, and how instructional spending relates to eighth-grade math growth. State and federal revenue are routed strongly to instruction, while local revenue is not. Leadership stability matters for what that spending achieves: instructional spending is modestly positively related to achievement in districts with stable leadership and negatively related in districts with high administrator turnover. Money matters, but its effect depends on the organizational capacity to use it well. These are preliminary results from regression models with district and year fixed effects, not causal estimates.

"Cannot Read a Word: Predictors of Non-Reading and the Literacy Gap in Rural Bangladesh"

Sarker, M. J. A., Sagar, M. S. U., Tabassum, A., & Islam, T.

Using baseline data from USAID’s Esho Shikhi project (1,232 students in 100 rural government schools), this paper asks which factors are associated with whether a child can read any word at all, and how these differ from the factors associated with overall literacy and fluency. In the sample, 28.4 percent of children read no word correctly. Two-level, survey-weighted models and a two-part decomposition show that home reading, parental academic support, and lower absenteeism predict crossing the threshold but not the literacy composite, while household wealth and teacher GESI implementation predict the composite but not the threshold. School size and lost learning during school closures operate only at the threshold, whereas overage-for-grade operates at both stages. These preliminary results are associational, not causal.

"If at First You Don't Succeed: The Impact of Failing the First Attempt on a Teacher Licensure Exam on Licensure, Employment, and Value-Added"

McGee, J. B., Sarker, M. J. A., Zamarro, G., & Camp, A.

Using a sharp regression discontinuity design, we estimate how failing the first attempt at a teacher licensure exam affects teacher supply and teaching effectiveness in Arkansas. We find no evidence that failing the exam changes the chance of becoming licensed and teaching in an Arkansas school. Failing does create a temporary hurdle to licensure for middle school, math, and STEM teachers, which diminishes over time, while passing on the first attempt has a negative long-term effect on teacher supply in science and social science. Neither passing nor failing has a statistically significant effect on students’ value-added.

Presented under evolving titles at AEFP 2024, SREE 2024, and APPAM 2024.
